# The Persona: Sarah Chen

**Who she is:**
- 44 years old. Principal of Chen Legal, a boutique commercial law firm in Melbourne's CBD
- 6 people total: Sarah (principal), 2 senior associates, 1 junior associate, 1 practice manager, 1 part-time admin assistant
- 11 years running her own firm. Before that, 8 years at a mid-tier firm — she left because she wanted to serve SME clients the big firms wouldn't touch
- Specialty: commercial contracts, business structuring, and franchise agreements for SMEs

**What her practice looks like:**
- Revenue: $1.4M. Profit margin: thin — around 18% and shrinking
- Client base: 85 active clients, mostly small-to-medium business owners she's worked with for years
- Billing: mostly fixed-fee with some hourly. She moved to fixed-fee because clients demanded it, but it squeezed margins when work takes longer than estimated
- She still does substantive legal work every day — she's not just managing. She's in the documents, reviewing her associates' work, and meeting clients. The practice can't function without her judgment.

**Why she's the persona:**
- She's the decision-maker AND the practitioner (your "business owner as learner" constraint)
- Her work is structured enough for agents but judgment-heavy enough to need boundaries
- She's exactly the person being bombarded by AI vendors right now
- She already runs on "junior does, partner reviews" — she just hasn't applied that thinking to AI yet

## Empathy Map: Sarah Chen

### What Sarah THINKS

*"I can't keep doing this much volume and maintain quality. Something has to give."*

*"AI could help, but if it makes a mistake on a client matter and I don't catch it, that's my name, my PI insurance, my reputation."*

*"My associates are good but they need supervision. I'm reviewing everything anyway. Could AI do some of that first-pass work? Or would I just be reviewing AI output instead of associate output?"*

*"Every vendor who calls me says their tool will save me time. None of them can tell me where the BOUNDARY is — what it can do well, what it can't, and how I'd know if it went wrong."*

*"My practice manager keeps sending me articles about AI in law firms. I read the first paragraph and feel overwhelmed. I don't have time to become an AI expert on top of everything else."*

*"The clients who are asking me about AI clauses in contracts are the same ones who want me to use AI to give them cheaper fees. I feel caught between protecting quality and meeting price pressure."*

*"I built this practice on my expertise and my relationships. If I get AI wrong, I lose both."*

### What Sarah FEELS

**Overwhelmed.** Not by AI specifically — by the total weight of being responsible for everything. Client work, business development, team management, compliance, billing, and now AI decisions stacked on top. AI isn't her biggest problem; it's the latest problem she doesn't have capacity to think about properly.

**Skeptical but curious.** She's not anti-AI. She's used ChatGPT for drafting emails and basic research. It was useful for the first week, then she stopped because she couldn't verify the output fast enough and it didn't save her time once she added verification. She's tried two legal AI tools at free trials and found them either too basic or too opaque.

**Anxious about getting it wrong.** Not in a vague "AI might be bad" way. In a very specific "if an AI tool hallucinates a clause in a franchise agreement and my client signs it, I am personally liable" way. This isn't abstract risk — it's professional indemnity, it's her licence, it's her reputation in a tight professional community where everyone knows everyone.

**Guilty about not doing more.** Her associates are asking about AI tools. Her clients are asking about AI clauses. Her practice manager is pushing for AI adoption to reduce costs. She feels like she should be leading this, but she doesn't know enough to lead it. That gap between her expertise in law and her lack of expertise in AI creates a specific kind of professional discomfort — she's used to being the most knowledgeable person in the room on her subject, and now she's not.

**Hopeful underneath it all.** She can see the potential. She's watched a senior associate spend 45 minutes formatting a contract that could be done in 5 if the content was right. She's seen her practice manager spend hours on compliance documentation that's formulaic. She knows there's value there. She just can't see how to get it without the risk.

### What Sarah SAYS

To her associates: *"Let's be careful with AI. I don't want anything going to a client that hasn't been through my review."*

To vendors who call: *"Send me an email with details. I'll look at it when I have time."* (She won't.)

To her practice manager: *"I know we need to look at this. Let me get through this month's billing first."*

To her peers at law society events: *"Is anyone actually using AI in their practice? Like, properly using it? Not just playing around?"* (This is her most honest question. She's looking for someone who's done it safely, who can tell her it's possible.)

To her partner at home: *"I spent three hours tonight reviewing documents I shouldn't have to review anymore. The associates are good but they're not ready for unsupervised work. And now I'm supposed to figure out AI as well?"*

### What Sarah DOES

- Subscribes to two legal technology newsletters and skims the headlines. Saves articles she never reads.
- Has a free ChatGPT account she uses occasionally for email drafting and basic research. Feels guilty she's not using it more.
- Tried two legal-specific AI tools on free trials. Cancelled both within the trial period — one because it was too limited, one because she couldn't understand how it reached its conclusions.
- Reviews every piece of work that leaves the firm. Every contract, every letter, every advice. Associates draft, Sarah reviews. She doesn't trust her junior associate's work without review yet, and she can't imagine trusting AI output without review.
- Works 55-60 hours a week. About 30% on substantive client work she enjoys and is excellent at, 40% on work she'd rather delegate (review, revision, compliance documentation, formatting), 30% on business operations and management.
- Has a printed quote on her wall from when she started the firm: *"Good law, well done."* It reminds her why she does this. She worries that "efficient" is starting to matter more than "good" in her own mind.
- Has not attended any AI workshops or courses. Has considered it. Hasn't made time.

### What Sarah SEES

**In her practice:**
- Stacks of client files that need review. Always more than she can get to.
- Associates producing good work that still needs her review — she's the bottleneck and she knows it.
- A practice manager who's efficient but overwhelmed by compliance documentation.
- A billing target she's tracking against every week. Hours she can't bill because she's doing work that could be someone else's.
- Her own calendar: client meetings, associate reviews, business development, and now "look into AI" on a perpetual to-do list that never gets to the top.

**In her industry:**
- Big firms publishing glossy AI policies that she can't replicate with 6 people.
- Legal tech startups promising the world and delivering tools she can't evaluate.
- Professional indemnity insurers starting to ask about AI governance on renewal forms.
- Law societies running "AI in Practice" seminars that are either too basic or too vendor-driven.

**In her inbox:**
- 5-10 AI vendor emails per week, all saying similar things, none addressing her actual concern
- Occasional thoughtful articles from legal publications that she bookmarks and doesn't read
- Internal emails from associates asking about tools she hasn't had time to evaluate

### What Sarah PAINS

**The bottleneck problem.** She IS the bottleneck. Every substantive piece of work passes through her review. She can't grow the practice without hiring more senior staff (expensive, hard to find), and she can't delegate more without quality risk. AI could help, but she can't see how to trust it.

**The verification problem.** She doesn't know how to verify AI output efficiently. Reviewing AI output takes almost as long as reviewing associate output, and she doesn't have a framework for deciding what to trust and what to question. Without that framework, AI doesn't save her time — it just changes whose output she's reviewing.

**The governance gap.** She knows she needs an AI policy. She knows her PI insurer will ask. She knows her professional association will issue guidance soon. But every AI governance template she's found is written for firms of 100+ people. She needs something she can actually implement with 6 people.

**The identity tension.** She built her practice on being the expert. AI threatens her sense of what makes her valuable. If AI can do the drafting, what's she for? Intellectually she knows it's judgment — but emotionally, she's been defined by her ability to produce the work, not just judge it. That's a harder shift than any vendor appreciates.

**The time poverty.** She doesn't have a half-day to spare for a workshop without being confident it's worth it. She doesn't have evenings to study AI. She doesn't have bandwidth to evaluate five different tools. Whatever the answer is, it has to meet her where she is — overloaded and cautious.

### What Sarah GAINS

If someone could give Sarah a framework that let her:

- **See exactly which tasks in her practice AI should handle, which she should keep, and where the boundary is** — she'd feel relief immediately. Not because AI is doing the work, but because she'd know WHERE it's safe to let go.

- **Verify AI output in less time than it takes to review associate work** — because she has a structured verification protocol, not just "read it and hope I catch everything." This is the unlock. If verification takes as long as doing it herself, there's no value.

- **Hand her PI insurer and law society a document that says "here's how we govern AI in this practice"** — that's not a theoretical benefit. That's a practical, risk-reducing, business-protecting artifact.

- **Sleep at night knowing her name is only on work she's actually verified** — this is the emotional core. It's not about efficiency. It's about professional identity and integrity.

- **Free up 30% of her time from mechanical review to spend on the work she's actually excellent at** — complex client advisory, business strategy, the judgment calls that only she can make. The work that made her start the practice in the first place.

## What I Notice About Sarah

A few things stand out that I want to name because they shape what we design for her:

**1. She doesn't need to be convinced AI is valuable.** She already knows. She's tried it. The gap isn't awareness — it's framework. She needs structure for thinking about AI, not a pitch for why AI matters.

**2. Her biggest fear isn't that AI will be bad.** It's that AI will be good *enough to look right but wrong in a way she might not catch.* Hallucination in a contract clause, not in a marketing email. The risk isn't total failure — it's plausible-looking error.

**3. She's looking for a peer who's done this safely.** Not a vendor. Not a consultant who's never practiced law. A peer — or someone who speaks her language — who can say "here's how we did it, here's where we were careful, here's what we learned." That's the trust gap.

**4. Her identity is wrapped up in this.** She's not just a business owner evaluating a tool. She's a professional whose entire sense of value is tied to her expertise. Any framework that positions AI as replacing what she does will trigger resistance — even if she agrees intellectually. The framework needs to position AI as *amplifying her judgment*, not substituting for it.

**5. She has 30 minutes, not a half-day, for the first conversation.** The workshop comes AFTER trust. The first touchpoint needs to be shorter, sharper, and immediately valuable.
